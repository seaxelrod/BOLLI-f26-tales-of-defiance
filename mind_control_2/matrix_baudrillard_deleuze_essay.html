Early in The Matrix, Neo opens a hollowed-out book to fetch some illicit software for a customer. The book is Jean Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation.1 The cameo is a deliberate wink. The Wachowskis asked their lead actors to read Baudrillard before filming, and later Morpheus greets Neo in the ruined real world with a line borrowed from the book: “Welcome to the desert of the real.”

The film draws on Baudrillard openly. A second thinker, Gilles Deleuze, never appears on screen, yet he explains the film’s machinery just as well. Read together, the two cover both halves of the Matrix. Baudrillard explains the illusion. Deleuze explains the system that runs it. Both help us ask the question at the heart of this course: what does defiance look like when control no longer feels like control?

Baudrillard: when the copy replaces the real

Baudrillard argued that modern life, saturated by media, advertising, and entertainment, has produced what he called hyperreality. Images and signs used to point to something real behind them. Now, he said, they mostly point to other images and signs, and the copy comes to feel more real than any original. He illustrated this with a fable about a map so detailed that it covers the entire territory. Eventually the map is all anyone sees, and the land underneath rots away unnoticed. The rotting land is his “desert of the real.”2

The Matrix turns this idea into a literal story: a perfect simulation of 1999 that the people inside take for reality.

Control through comfort. Baudrillard is especially useful for thinking about mind control. In his account, control doesn’t work mainly through force or censorship, as it does in 1984. It works by surrounding people with so many signs that no solid ground remains from which to question them. The Matrix pacifies humanity by giving it a believable, reasonably pleasant world, not by chaining its mind. Cypher makes the point unforgettable. Sitting in the simulation with a steak he knows isn’t real, he decides he would rather go back in: “Ignorance is bliss.” People may prefer the illusion. Control rests on consent and comfort, not on coercion.

Where the film and the philosopher part ways. Baudrillard himself said the film misunderstood him. In a 2004 interview he called it the kind of film about the Matrix that the Matrix itself could have produced.3 His objection was that the movie keeps an old-fashioned structure, going back to Plato’s cave. A real world (the ruined surface, the city of Zion) exists behind the illusion, and anyone brave enough to take the red pill can wake up into it.

For Baudrillard, there is no such exit. Hyperreality has no “real” waiting outside. On this view, the heroic story of awakening is itself a comforting myth, one more product the system sells us. Defiance becomes bleaker and stranger. It looks less like revolution and more like refusal: indifference, silence, gestures the system cannot absorb or answer.

Deleuze: from walls to codes

Baudrillard describes what has happened to reality. Deleuze, in a short 1990 essay called “Postscript on the Societies of Control,” describes how power now operates.4

He started from the work of Michel Foucault, who described disciplinary societies.5 In these, power works through enclosed institutions: the prison, school, factory, hospital, barracks. Each one shapes you inside its walls, and you pass from one enclosure to the next over a lifetime. Big Brother’s Oceania is discipline taken to its extreme.

Deleuze argued that we were moving into societies of control. Power no longer needs walls. It works through continuous, flexible monitoring: credit scores, passwords, endless training and retraining, ongoing performance reviews. Discipline is a mold: it presses you into a fixed shape. Control is modulation: it adjusts itself to you, constantly and invisibly.

Several features of the film fit this picture closely.

The pods versus the simulation. The pods where human bodies lie are pure enclosure, as confining as any prison. But the real work of control happens inside the simulation, where people move freely and feel unconfined. That is Deleuze’s central point. The most effective control is the kind that never feels like confinement.

Codes and access. Deleuze says that what matters in a control society isn’t a signature or an ID number marking you as an individual. What matters is a code that grants or denies access. The film’s plot turns on exactly this: the access codes to Zion’s mainframe locked in Morpheus’s head, the crew hacking in and out, the ringing phones that serve as exits.

“Dividuals.” Deleuze suggests the individual gets broken down into dividuals: data points, accounts, samples, streams to be managed. The image of human beings farmed as batteries is a brutal, literal version of this. Each person has been reduced to an energy output.

The Agents. Agent Smith can take over anyone’s body at any moment. Control here isn’t located in a guard or a wall. It can surface anywhere in the network. Deleuze contrasted the old disciplinary society, a burrowing mole that moves from enclosure to enclosure, with the new control society, a serpent that coils and slides through everything. The Agents are serpents.

What Deleuze adds on defiance

Here the two thinkers differ most sharply. Baudrillard leaves little room for resistance, since there is no outside to escape to. Deleuze is less fatalistic. He wrote that there is no need to fear or to hope, only to look for new weapons. Elsewhere he suggested that resistance might take the form of “circuit breakers”: small pockets of silence and non-communication that escape the network’s constant monitoring.6

Neo and his crew fit this picture well. They don’t attack the Matrix from some pure outside. They are hackers who use the system’s own codes against it, bending its rules from within. On Deleuze’s reading, the film’s real hope lies in the skill to find the cracks in a system that was never going to disappear, rather than in a total awakening.

Questions for discussion

  • Cypher chooses the comfortable illusion. Is his choice cowardly, or simply honest?
  • Baudrillard says there is no “real world” to wake up into. If he’s right, what would defiance even mean?
  • Deleuze says control no longer needs walls. Where in your own life are you monitored or modulated without ever being confined?
  • What might a “circuit breaker” look like today?

Extra Credit Appendix on the sequel “The Matrix Reloaded”

The first sequel, The Matrix Reloaded (2003), moves the story much closer to both thinkers.

Near the end of the film, Neo meets the Architect, the program that designed the Matrix. The Architect explains that this is not the first Matrix but the sixth. Each earlier version failed because some humans rejected it. The machines’ solution was to build rejection into the system. “The One” and the free city of Zion are themselves part of the design. They are a planned outlet that gathers the rebels in one place, and the machines periodically wipe it out and restart the cycle.

The Baudrillard reading. This is close to Baudrillard’s warning that there is no outside. The red pill, the awakening, and the resistance turn out to belong to the simulation’s own machinery. The escape route was a product of the system all along.

The Deleuze reading. Six versions of the Matrix, each adjusted to absorb the last “anomaly,” are modulation in its purest form. The system doesn’t crush deviation; it learns from it and updates. Resistance becomes data for the next version.

Question for discussion: If control can absorb every act of resistance and use it to improve itself, what would a genuinely “new weapon,” in Deleuze’s phrase, look like?
