For most of the modern era, power worked by putting you somewhere. You moved through a sequence of enclosed institutions — family, then school, then the army or the job, with the hospital or the prison waiting if things went wrong. Each had walls, a schedule, and a set of rules that stopped at the door. You finished one and entered the next. Michel Foucault called this **discipline**.[^1]

In 1990, before the web existed, a French philosopher named Gilles Deleuze wrote five pages arguing that this arrangement was already ending, and that something harder to see was replacing it. He called it **control**.[^2] Three of his images are worth attention in our course.

**The mold and the modulation.** An enclosure is a mold: it stamps you into shape, then releases you. Control is a modulation — a cast that keeps reshaping itself, adjusting continuously. Nothing is ever finished. The diploma gives way to perpetual retraining; the exam gives way to permanent assessment.

**The individual and the "dividual."** Discipline knew you by your signature and your number — a whole person, filed in a place. Control does not need the whole person. It needs your parts: your purchases, your location, your dwell time, your score. You are divisible, and the pieces are recombined into markets and samples. What grants or refuses you access is no longer a guard at a gate but a code.

**The mole and the serpent.** The mole burrows through fixed tunnels — enclosed, discontinuous, always starting over. The serpent coils continuously through open ground, everywhere and nowhere in particular. Deleuze ends the essay by observing that a serpent's coils are more intricate than a mole's burrows.

**A question to hold.** Discipline forbids. Control does not need to forbid anything, because it can simply make one path frictionless and the others tedious.

> How would you know if you were being controlled by something that never once told you no?
