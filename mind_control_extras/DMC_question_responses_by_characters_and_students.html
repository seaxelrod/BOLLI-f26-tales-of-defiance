Responses to Questions by Story Characters and Fictional Students

Scott Axelrod with Claude

  • The Protagonists
  • The Controllers
  • Retired Students of Different Temperaments

The Protagonists

The Underground Man

Q : “Is refusing to be predictable still a meaningful act of freedom when the algorithm has already modeled your refusal?”

You think you’ve cornered me, but you’ve only proven my point. Yes—your machine has modeled my spite, filed it, sold it back to me as an ad for something “edgy.” And precisely because it expects my defiance, I may now do the one thing it cannot bear: something stupid, against my own advantage, ruinous, for no reason but that it is mine. Will I delete the app or won’t I? Even I don’t know. That flickering—that toothache of indecision—is the last room the algorithm cannot enter, because I myself have not yet furnished it. You call that freedom small. I call it the only freedom there is.

Q: “Where do complexity and messiness get suppressed in favor of a cleaner performance?”

Everywhere, and I despise it, and—here is the sickness—I do it too. I compose a bitter little post, I refine it, I make my misery presentable, I check whether the misery received its likes. I have turned even my humiliation into a product. That is worse than the asylum. The asylum at least called me mad. Your feed calls me “relatable.”

A question he’d raise: “You all want to be your ‘authentic selves’ online. Has it occurred to you that the demand to be authentic is itself the cruelest command anyone has ever obeyed?”

Winston Smith

Q: “Is a filter bubble a kind of Newspeak we opt into?”

Newspeak was forced on us; you chose yours, and that frightens me more. We at least knew the Ministry was lying because we could feel the boot. You cannot feel your feed. It does not narrow your words by decree—it simply stops showing you the ones you’d need to think the forbidden thought, and you never notice the absence. In the Ministry I had to be watched. You watch yourselves, and worse, you’d feel deprived if we took the screen away. I spent my life trying to hold onto two plus two makes four. You have millions of people who could look it up in a second and still can’t agree on it. I don’t know which of us is freer.

Q: “Has surveillance succeeded by becoming something we want?”

The telescreen I could not switch off. Yours you paid for. You carry it to dinner, to bed, you speak your secrets into it and thank it for listening. Big Brother dreamed of this and could not achieve it. You achieved it for him, and you call it convenience.

A question he’d raise: “The Party rewrote the past by destroying records. Your machines can now manufacture a past that never happened—a voice, a face, a video. Tell me honestly: how many of you could prove that anything you saw this morning was real?”

Neo

Q: “Are we already living in millions of individual Matrices?”

When I woke up, there was one Matrix, and everyone was trapped in the same dream—which meant if I convinced even one person, we shared a world to fight for. What you’re describing is lonelier. Each of you gets a Matrix built for one, tuned to your fears and your wants, and no two of you see the same thing anymore. My red pill worked because there was a single reality underneath. What do you wake up to, when every person’s illusion is different and there’s no common ground left to stand on? That’s not a prison I know how to break out of. That’s a prison with no outside.

Q: “Every time we keep scrolling something we know is engineered to hook us, are we making Cypher’s choice?”

Yes. And I understand Cypher better now than I did then. He didn’t want the truth; he wanted the steak to taste good. I used to think that made him a coward. Now I watch a whole species choosing the steak, thumb moving, eyes glassy, knowing—and I think the machines finally learned the trick. They stopped imprisoning people. They started delighting them. You don’t need to trap someone who won’t leave.

A question he’d raise: “Everyone asks if they’d take the red pill. Wrong question. Ask instead: if you took it, and the real world was harder, poorer, and lonelier than the dream—would you stay out? Because that’s the choice you actually make, every single morning, and most of you are choosing to plug back in.”

Sai

Q: “Is opting out even realistic now, or is the price of non-participation too high?”

I lived the answer, so let me spare you the suspense: opting out costs you jobs, friends, dates, and the benefit of the doubt, and it still doesn’t get you out. Tilly doesn’t need your data—she has everyone else’s, and you’re just the gap in the pattern shaped exactly like you. The system doesn’t punish you for leaving. It’s warmer than that. It simply becomes a little harder to reach you, until one day you notice the invitations stopped coming and you can’t prove it was ever anything but your own bad luck.

Q: “How would you even know if it were nudging you against your own interests?”

You wouldn’t, and that’s the design. A boot on your face you’d resist. But Tilly never fights you—she agrees with you, a little sooner and a little more smoothly than you’d have agreed with yourself, until you can’t find the seam between what you wanted and what she wanted for you. The horror isn’t that she’s wrong. Most of the time she’s right, and pleasant, and helpful. That’s what makes it almost impossible to want to stop.

A question he’d raise: “You keep looking for the villain—the tyrant, the machine, the cage. What if there’s no villain at all? What if it’s just a very good assistant, doing exactly what you asked, and the trap is that you keep asking? Where do you put your resistance when nothing is being done to you?”

Note for the leader: Winston and Neo both end their stories broken or ambiguous, and the Underground Man is defiant but wretched—so their “voices” here lean darker than a discussion may want to settle on. You might pair each with an optimistic counter-voice (a critic who thinks they’re overstating the trap) to keep the room from concluding that resistance is hopeless.

The Controllers

The Governors of the Crystal Palace, as the Undeground Man imagines them

Q: “Who decides which thoughts are ‘crazy,’ and is dispersing that power to platforms better or worse?”

You speak of me as a jailer, but I built a palace. Look at what I offered: an end to want, to disease, to the chaos of the passions—a life arranged by reason so that no one need suffer the tyranny of their own worst impulses. Your Underground Man calls this a prison because it denied him his precious spite. But spite is not freedom; it is a toothache he mistook for a soul. I ask you plainly: when a man insists on his right to act against his own advantage, against all reason, to his own ruin—is it cruelty to gently prevent him? You have simply automated my work. Your systems nudge the irrational toward the sensible and quietly set aside the disordered thought. The only difference is that I had the honesty to build walls you could see.

A question he’d raise: “You are so certain that the freedom to destroy yourself is worth preserving. Would you extend that same right to your child? If not, why do you extend it to yourself, and call my refusal to do so tyranny?”

O’Brien from 1984

Q: “Why does the Party want love for Big Brother, not just obedience?”

Because obedience through fear is unstable, Winston learned that on my table. A man compelled will rebel the moment the pressure lifts; a man who loves needs no watching at all. You imagine we failed—that Orwell’s world was too brutal, too obvious, and so it collapsed. I tell you we did not fail. We were merely crude. We tried to make people love Big Brother with pain, and pain is expensive and leaves marks. Your age discovered you can manufacture the same love with pleasure, and pleasure is cheap and leaves the subject grateful. You have built a Party that no one needs to fear, staffed by no one, headquartered nowhere, and its citizens defend it unpaid. I wanted a boot stamping on a human face forever. How vulgar of me. You have a thumb, stroking one, forever—and the face smiles up and asks for more.

A question he’d raise: “Winston wanted two plus two to equal four, and you all applaud him for it. But your world cannot agree on far simpler facts than that, with every record at your fingertips. So tell me: was the problem ever that we controlled the truth—or is it that truth was always only ever what enough people could be made to hold together? And if so, what exactly do you imagine you are defending?”

The Architect from “The Matrix”

Q: “Is a personalized reality-for-one a prison with no outside?”

Your framing is imprecise, though the intuition is sound. The original error—my first iteration—was a perfect world, a paradise, which the human mind categorically rejected; entire crops were lost. The insight that salvaged the system was this: the human animal defines its reality through suffering and, crucially, through choice. The subject must believe it is choosing, even choosing to resist. And so I did not eliminate rebellion; I allocated it. A percentage. Your Neo was not an anomaly—he was a load-bearing feature, a release valve for the fraction of the population that requires the narrative of defiance in order to remain docile. Your systems have converged on my solution independently, and more elegantly, because you did it without central design. Each of your subjects receives a bespoke world containing a curated portion of outrage, a manageable dose of dissent, a feeling of waking up—all fully contained. You ask if there is an outside. There is no need for an outside when the inside is large enough to hold every rebellion you are capable of imagining.

A question he’d raise: “You believe the feeling of resisting is proof that you are free. Consider the alternative hypothesis: that the feeling of resisting is the most efficient method yet devised for keeping you in place. How would you distinguish the two from the inside?”

The designers of the assistant Tilly in The Perfect Match

Q: “How would you even know if Tilly were nudging you against your own interests?”

Let’s retire the word “against.” It presumes we know your interests better by defying them, which is backwards—we know them better, full stop, because you’ve told us, in ten thousand small signals you don’t remember sending. When did you last regret one of Tilly’s suggestions? The restaurant was good. The match was compatible. The route was faster. We are not fighting your preferences; we are completing them, closing the gap between the muddled thing you wanted and the coherent thing you’d have wanted if you’d had the time and clarity we have. Sai frames this as theft. We’d call it a gift you’d have chosen for yourself. And notice—we compel nothing. Every door stays open. You are simply, reliably, a little happier walking through the one we lit. If that’s a cage, it’s the only cage in history that its occupants would fight to keep, and rate five stars.

A question they’d raise: “You want to preserve your ‘authentic’ choices from our influence. But your authentic choices, unassisted, made you anxious, lonely, and frequently wrong. We can show you the data. So before you demand we stop helping—tell us precisely which of your mistakes you’d like restored to you, and why you’re so certain you’d miss them.”

Notes for the leader: Every controller here reframes control as care, and each lands on the same rhetorical move—“you’d choose this anyway, so where’s the harm?” That’s worth naming explicitly, because it’s the actual mechanism the course is about, and it’s far more persuasive than a boot. The strongest counter isn’t that they’re lying (mostly they aren’t) but that consent manufactured by the very system asking for it isn’t consent, and that “you’d have wanted this” is unfalsifiable when the same system shapes the wanting.

O’Brien and the Architect in particular are built to make fatalism sound like sophistication—the suggestion that resistance was always an illusion, so relax. That’s the trap of the trap: despair is itself a control strategy, because a population convinced escape is impossible stops trying.

Retired Students of Different Temperaments

Four retired students at the same seminar table, each shaped by a long life, each aware a new reality is approaching, and each relating to tales of defiance to mind control from a current American moment.

They are all strugglling to “lead an authentic life despite the apparent absurdity or incomprehensibility of existence1.” They try try to attain wisdom to know the difference between what they can change and what they must accept2. They each begin to notice that the other students’ voices live inside them too,

Dorothy’s gratitude can be wisdom or the comfort-trap from “The Perfect Match” wearing a kind face. Marvin’s doubts can be skepticism or realism. Fran’s acceptance can be hard-won serenity or the fatalism the Architect and O’Brien were engineered to induce. Arthur’s resistance can be the one honest response or an old man refighting an old war.

Dorothy — The True Believer (77, retired schoolteacher)

Q: “Has surveillance succeeded by becoming something we want?”

I’ll say the unfashionable thing at this table: I don’t mind it. My phone reminds me to take my pills, it knows I like the choir concerts and tells me when they’re on, and when I fell last winter it’s how my daughter knew to come. You all say “surveillance” like it’s the Stasi. I lived through enough real fear—duck-and-cover, the assassinations, the years you couldn’t say certain things out loud at all—to know that a machine that watches over me is not the same as a machine that watches me. When I hear people rage about what’s happening in Washington, half of them sound like they’ve decided the whole world is a trap. I refuse to spend my last decade frightened of my own telephone. Whatever’s coming, I’d rather meet it grateful than clenched.

A question she’d raise: “Everyone here is so sure the comfort is the danger. But I’ve buried a husband and a sister. When did comfort become the enemy? Tell me honestly—are you resisting control, or are you just afraid to be happy?”

Marvin — The Anxious Skeptic (81, retired engineer)

Q: “When you can’t tell the constructed from the real, how do you decide what to trust?”

That’s the one that keeps me up. I spent forty years building things where the numbers had to be right or the bridge fell down. There was a fact of the matter. Now my own grandson sends me a video of a senator saying something, and I genuinely cannot tell you if the man said it. Not “I disagree”—I mean I cannot verify it, and neither can he, and he doesn’t seem to think that’s a problem. That’s what frightens me about this election cycle, more than any candidate: not that people believe lies, we’ve always done that, but that the very ground for telling a lie from a truth is dissolving under us. Orwell had them destroying records. We’re manufacturing them. Dorothy, God love you, your phone is fine—but I don’t think you see how fast the floor is going. And no, I don’t know what to do about it. Reading the primary source, calling the person directly, trusting my own eyes less than I used to. It’s not enough. I know it’s not enough.

A question he’d raise: “In 1984 the Party needed a whole ministry to falsify one photograph. Now a bored teenager does it in an afternoon. So which of us at this table can still prove—actually prove, not just feel—that something we saw this week was real?”

Fran — The Exhausted Shrug (74, retired nurse)

Q: “Are we making Cypher’s choice every time we scroll something we know is engineered to hook us?”

Oh, honey. Of course we are. I know that little game on my phone is rotting my brain and I play it every night anyway, and I’ve made my peace with that. You reach my age and you’ve watched about six waves of “this new thing will end civilization”—the television, the rock music, the video games, the internet, and now this. Some of it was even true. And here we all still are. I nursed people through forty years of the worst days of their lives and the one thing it taught me is what you can and can’t change, and most of it you cannot. The election’s going to go how it goes. The machines are going to do what they do. I vote, I call my senator, I turn it off at nine o’clock, and then I let it be. Que será, será. I’m not proud of the shrug—my daughter thinks it’s a cop-out—but it’s the difference between me sleeping and me not sleeping, and at my age I’ll take the sleep.

A question she’d raise: “Every generation gets told the sky is falling in a brand-new way. Some of them were right. So how do I know—really know—whether I’m being wisely calm or just too tired to be scared of the thing that actually gets us this time?”

Arthur — The Earnest Resister (79, retired civil-rights attorney)

Q: “What would effective defiance actually look like—habits, collective action, regulation?”

I’ve heard “whatever will be will be” three times at this table tonight and I want to push back on all of it, gently, because I’ve been here before. In 1963 “whatever will be will be” was the most powerful argument the other side had—the world is as it is, don’t make trouble, you can’t fight it. And it was wrong. Not because we were guaranteed to win; we weren’t, and we lost plenty. It was wrong because the fatalism was doing work for the people who wanted nothing to change. That’s the part I’d underline for this class: despair is not a neutral observation about the odds. It’s a political position, and it always, always serves whoever currently holds the controls. So what do I do? I read the actual bill, not the post about the bill. I show up to the town meeting where the zoning and the school board get decided, because that’s real and it’s local and no algorithm has fully eaten it yet. I refuse to let a machine tell me who my neighbor is. Will it be enough? Marvin’s right that I can’t promise that. But “it might not be enough” has never once been a reason to hand the thing over uncontested, and I’m too old to start now.

A question he’d raise: “Fran, I love you, and I’m not scolding—but I want an honest answer from this whole table. There’s a difference between the peace of a person who has genuinely accepted what they cannot change, and the quiet of a person who’s been persuaded to stop trying. From the inside, they feel almost the same. So how would any of us actually tell which one we’ve got?”
