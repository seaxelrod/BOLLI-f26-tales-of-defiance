Definition of Doublethink in the Book 1984,

“Doublethink means the power of holding two contradictory beliefs in one’s mind simultaneously, and accepting both of them. … This process has to be conscious, or it would not be carried out with sufficient precision. But it also has to be unconscious, or it would bring with it a feeling of falsity and hence of guilt.”

IV: 1984 Movie vs Book

from What are the main differences between the film and novel 1984?

The 1984 film is noted for being closer to the original book, though there are still a few differences. This version creates a distinct hand salute for the Party, evoking Nazi Germany, and the Party’s members also refer to one another as “brother” and “sister” rather than the more overtly Soviet “comrade” as in the novel. Otherwise, this version retains Orwell’s bleak finale.

from Novel vs. Movie - 1984

Whenever a novel is translated into a movie, the director might decide to change the plot to fit his understanding and interpretation of the book. In the movie adaption of 1984, the director followed the novel fairly well with no drastic changes. However, here are three differences between the movie and the novel:

In the novel, the interactions between Winston and Julia are spread out over many months. They are unable to meet frequently. The book draws out the details about how Winston and Julia try to meet up. It isn’t until a couple months later that Winston and Julia unite at the second story of Mr. Charrington’s building. Yet, in the movie, it appears that everything is hastened; Winston and Julia quickly move into the building, skipping a lot of the previous encounters. This was probably done because the movie is a condensed version, so the individual encounters were removed because they are insignificant to the overall plot.

One of the starkest differences between the movie and the novel occurs when Winston goes to meet O’Brien. In the novel, Winston goes with Julia, and they talk in length with O’Brien about the Brotherhood. He questions them and warns them about the consequences of joining the Brotherhood. However, in the movie, only Winston goes to meet O’Brien. The meeting was quick and brief. O’Brien merely suggests that he reads Goldstein’s book. I felt that this was an unnecessary omission because without the detailed encounter, I was slightly confused with the conversion in the movie.

In the novel, the diary entries are all at the beginning of the novel. The director decided to integrate the journal entries into the story, which I thought was a good idea. After a few scenes, there is a scene of Winston writing in the diary. The diary entries are all the same, but when they are written in relation to other events are different.

V: Commentary on 1984

Afterword to the 1961 Signet edition of 1984, by Eric Fromm,

  • website copy of PDF

Claude AI Summary of Fromm’s Afterward

Fromm wrote this afterword in 1961 for the Signet edition, and his main argument is that 1984 is both a mood and a warning. The mood is near-despair about humanity’s future. The warning is that people may become soulless automatons without ever noticing it has happened.

From utopia to negative utopia. Fromm sets the novel inside a 2,000-year Western tradition of hope in human progress. That tradition runs through the Hebrew prophets, Christian thought, and Renaissance utopias such as More’s Utopia, Campanella’s City of the Sun, and Andreae’s Christianopolis, and it continues up to Bellamy’s Looking Backward. He traces the collapse of this hope to the period from World War I onward: Stalin’s betrayal of socialism, the Depression, Nazism, the bombing of civilian cities, and finally the hydrogen bomb. Zamyatin’s We, Huxley’s Brave New World, and 1984 form a new trilogy of “negative utopias” that mirrors the old positive one. He calls the shift a historical paradox. Early modern people were hopeful even though they lacked the means to end scarcity and war. Modern people have those means and are losing hope just as it becomes realizable.

The shared question. All three books ask whether human nature can be changed so completely that people forget their longing for freedom, dignity, love, and truth. Fromm notes that none of the authors treats human nature as a blank slate. The extreme methods each one needs to crush it (lobotomy in We, eugenics and drugs in Huxley, torture and brainwashing in Orwell) actually show how strong those human strivings are. Still, all three conclude that dehumanization is possible with techniques already known.

What Orwell specifically contributes: - Permanent war. Ongoing arms production props up the economy, and a society that is always preparing for annihilation cannot stay democratic. Fromm turns this directly against Cold War deterrence: the arms race would destroy freedom even if the bombs never fell. - The abolition of objective truth. The Party holds that reality exists only in the mind, so controlling minds means controlling truth. Fromm compares O’Brien’s interrogation of Winston to Dostoevsky’s Grand Inquisitor, with one difference: the Party doesn’t even claim to want people’s happiness. It wants power for its own sake. He links this to Alan Harrington’s idea of corporate “mobile truth,” where a product is “the best” for as long as you work for the company that makes it. In such a system, a minority of one has to conclude that he is insane. - Doublethink is already here. Fromm’s examples are Western. The “free world” includes dictatorships such as Franco’s and Salazar’s. Herman Kahn’s nuclear strategy calmly expects tens of millions of deaths followed by “normal and happy lives,” and that reasoning is called “sober.” Christian nations plan mass killing with few qualms. - Successful manipulation means people believe the lie. They aren’t saying the opposite of what they think. They actually think the opposite of what is true, so “Slavery is Freedom” feels like freedom. The West also falsifies its own ideology when it calls a centralized, bureaucratic, materialist managerial society “individualist.”

Conclusion. Fromm doubts that a fully dehumanized society could even survive, because it would be a world of madmen, hinted at in the “mad gleam” in the Party leader’s eye. He reads Orwell as a warner, not a prophet of doom. The real danger is managerial industrialism itself, which “builds machines which act like men and develops men who act like machines,” and it applies under any ideology. Orwell’s hope is a desperate one, and it depends on readers recognizing that the book is about us, not only about Stalinism.

A paragraph from Afterward that Connects Doublethink of Corporatism and Rejection of Outliers

If I work for a big corporation which claims that its product is better than that of all competitors, the question whether this claim is justified or not in terms of ascertainable reality becomes irrelevant. What matters is that as long as I serve this particular corporation, this claim becomes “my” truth, and I decline to examine whether it is an objectively valid truth. In fact, if I change my job and move over to the corporation which was until now “my” competitor, I shall accept the new truth, that its product is the best, and subjectively speaking, this new truth will be as true as the old one. It is one of the most characteristic and destructive developments of our own society that man, becoming more and more of an instrument, transforms reality more and more into something relative to his own interests and functions. Truth is proven by the consensus of millions; to the slogan “how can millions be wrong” is added “and how can a minority of one be right.” Orwell shows quite clearly that in a system in which the concept of truth as an objective judgment concerning reality is abolished, anyone who is a minority of one must be convinced that he is insane.

Claude Summary of Not Death, but Annihilation: Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four and the Catastrophe of Englishness, Erik Jaccard, local copy

Erik Jaccard, “Not Death, but Annihilation: Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four and the Catastrophe of Englishness” (Critical Insights, 2016, pp. 98–112)

The argument in one line: 1984 is usually read as a universal warning about humanity under totalitarianism, but it is also an English catastrophe novel, and when you read it that way, the “humanity” Winston defends turns out to be English culture.

The target. Jaccard opens with Fromm’s Afterword as the example of the reading he wants to complicate. Fromm, and Trilling with his view of Orwell as a teller of truths above politics, treat the novel as a statement about “man” in modernity. Jaccard says this depends on reading the book through only one genre.

Two genres, two lenses. - Dystopia puts the society first and characters second. The protagonist does two jobs: he shows how the system works, and he carries a counter-narrative of resistance. Winston’s diary, the affair with Julia, and the Brotherhood all fit, and O’Brien’s “you are the last man” frames the stakes as humanity itself. - The English catastrophe novel emerged in late-Victorian anxiety about imperial decline, made sharper by Darwin’s suggestion that civilizations can regress as well as progress. Disaster shrinks the world down to England, strips survivors back to basics, and tests “Englishness” under pressure. These books often expose imperial hypocrisy, then end by rebuilding the new world on English values. The critique of empire quietly restores it.

Why 1984 qualifies. Oceania comes after nuclear war, so it is literally post-catastrophic. With the past erased, London is effectively an island. Unlike a typical dystopian hero, who is at home in his world, Winston looks backward like a catastrophe survivor. The key moment is the toast: offered the future, humanity, or the death of Big Brother, he chooses “the past.”

Englishness as “ancestral memory.” Jaccard reads Winston’s vague bodily sense that things were once different, his longing for the private room above Charrington’s shop, and the Golden Country alongside Orwell’s essay “England Your England.” In that essay, Englishness is a felt, unconscious continuity of privacy, gentleness, and individual liberty, living beneath the surface of official life. Winston feels freedom is his right not as a human being but because being free is what an Englishman naturally does. In Conor Cruise O’Brien’s critique, Orwell never seriously imagined being part of a people with a different history. So the novel’s universal defense of humanity is built on a parochial base.

The title. Winston and Julia believe “they can’t get inside you.” Orwell’s patriotic writing assumes the same thing: individuals die, but the English spirit persists, and a turnip seed never grows into a parsnip. Room 101 refutes this. Jaccard goes further than Jonathan Rose’s point that the horror is “there won’t always be an England.” The real despair, he argues, is that the cultural ground from which individual life grows is annihilated, and not merely killed. He adds that Winston’s resistance itself seems to have been engineered: the recurring dream of O’Brien’s voice, the pull toward the prole district, the exculpatory scrap of paper conveniently “slipped in.” The Party has co-opted the very instinct for Englishness that freedom depended on. That co-optation is the catastrophe.
