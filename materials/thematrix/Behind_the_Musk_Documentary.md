This is a 78-minute episode of *On with Kara Swisher* (posted October 8, 2026), in which Swisher interviews filmmaker Alex Gibney and Justine Musk, Elon Musk's first wife, about Gibney's nearly four-hour HBO documentary *Musk*. Swisher appears in the film herself. The summary follows the conversation in order and is based on YouTube's auto-generated captions, so a few names may be slightly off.

## Framing and why the film is four hours (0:00–3:00)

Swisher frames the episode around two questions: how a man known for missing his own deadlines became the world's first trillionaire, and why society let him gather so much power.

Gibney started the film in 2022. By early 2024 he had a cut of under two hours about Musk's economic power. Then came the attempted assassination of Trump and Musk's alignment with him. HBO asked Gibney not to finish the shorter film and to expand it instead. He admits he hadn't realized at first that Musk's political shift had really begun back in 2022.

## Justine Musk's perspective and why she spoke (3:00–7:20)

- Justine published three novels in her early 30s. She later moved into blogging and became unsure what kind of writer she wanted to be after the marriage.
- She knew Elon and his brother Kimbal in college. She describes herself as an outsider and observer in his world, a bit like living in a foreign country. She only came to understand that world after the divorce.
- She was surprised by Musk's turn to the right, and so was Swisher.
- She agreed to take part after long conversations with Gibney. She had three reasons:
  - to publicly support her daughter Jenna
  - to put certain things on the record
  - not to control the narrative, but to make sure it was one she could recognize. Both she and Swisher note that Musk tends to reshape stories to suit himself.

## The personal as political, and gaslighting (7:20–12:20)

Gibney says two things Justine told him shaped the film:

- **"The personal is political."** You have to understand Musk the person to understand his political journey.
- **She first learned about gaslighting from Elon.** Gibney saw this reality-bending, "science-fiction making" habit as the key to understanding Musk's business and political conduct.

Justine describes a moment during their separation when she tried to name what Musk did with the truth. It wasn't exactly lying. It was a constant reframing that twisted her words until the twist itself became the topic. She says it felt like having the rug pulled out over and over. She concluded that he communicates in order to control and dominate, especially with women.

Swisher widens this to Silicon Valley's habit of overselling products while hiding the harms. She adds that tech leaders accuse others of victim mentality while casting themselves as victims.

## How scared were they? (12:20–18:40)

**Gibney** says he wasn't scared at first. He had already investigated Scientology, the Catholic Church, Enron, and the U.S. government. That changed once Musk became a kind of "shadow president." His reasoning:

- Trump had turned the Justice Department into a tool for revenge, so the rule of law might not protect him.
- The civil legal system favors the rich.
- Musk has threatened to sue, and Gibney has taken precautions.
- Still, he thinks speaking openly is the best defense, because others agree with you and these men care what people think of them.

**Justine** says she is still nervous and calls Musk "a scary dude." She was also annoyed at the idea of being intimidated into silence in a free country. Other factors in her decision:

- years of ill health, possibly long COVID
- watching Jenna take public attacks alone

She gets emotional and pauses. She then explains that as a writer she is disturbed by how language and personal detail get flattened by algorithms. She doesn't think storytelling should be left to people trying to control the narrative rather than explore it.

## The digital Elon (20:37–26:10)

Musk refused to be interviewed. Gibney's team built a database of everything Musk had said publicly and created a computer-generated Musk to "answer" questions in the film. The audio is Musk's real recorded voice, not a synthetic clone.

Gibney has done similar things before:

- In *Client 9*, an actress performed an escort's transcribed interview.
- In *Zero Days*, a digitized composite character voiced NSA sources.

He chose a simulated interview because of Musk's obsession with simulation theory. The avatar sits in a gamer chair and looks straight into the camera. Gibney says viewers often forget it isn't real, which ties into how people form attachments to AI chatbots. He considers the approach journalistically defensible because every word is Musk's, and he admits the avatar is flattering.

Justine found it uncanny. The avatar reminded her of Musk's father, Errol. It also struck her that an AI built from someone's past words can't think anything new; the person's thinking is frozen in place. The two also briefly dismiss the right-wing idea that "empathy is the suicide of Western civilization."

## Media consolidation and distribution fears (26:10–30:50)

Swisher raises several pressures on the film's release:

- Reports that Universal was nervous about international distribution, which Universal denies.
- Larry Ellison, a close Musk friend, backing the Paramount–Warner Bros. merger. Warner Bros. owns HBO.
- A Semafor report that the Ellisons might ask Musk to help finance the merger.

Gibney says this shows how harmful media consolidation has become, whether through "soft" rules or outright censorship. His points:

- Universal now appears to be on board.
- He calls the merger a clear antitrust and free-speech violation that will go through anyway under a transactional Justice Department.
- He praises the current HBO team, who asked him to make the film longer and supported him through the Scientology fight. He still worries about what happens if Musk becomes a financial stakeholder.
- His safeguard is the theatrical release through Bleecker Street, because it is hard to put a film back in the box once audiences have seen it.
- He compares the situation to his earlier film *The Trials of Henry Kissinger*. No U.S. broadcaster would air it, but it ran for four months at Film Forum. He says theaters are again becoming a way to say what conflict-averse or propagandistic platforms won't.

## The Musk family dinner table (30:50–34:50)

Justine recalls family dinners during the marriage:

- Elon's sister Tosca joked at Kimbal's wedding that in the Musk family, women are in the same category as dogs and children: to be seen and not heard.
- The tables were dominated by men monologuing. She would visibly get bored, and they would be angry with her afterward.
- She ties this to politics. When only one group gets to tell the story, the map doesn't just get confused with the territory; it gets redrawn under your feet. In her view, democracy is a conversation that has to protect the voices that aren't dominant.

## The "engineer" image (34:53–37:20)

Swisher argues that Musk fetishizes being seen as an engineer even though he usually isn't the best technologist in the room. She contrasts him with Steve Jobs, who never claimed to be Wozniak.

Justine says Musk's real genius is combining ideas and people from different fields and driving them hard toward a result. Swisher calls this more like Henry Ford than Edison. Justine thinks "tech" is the highest status in his world and fits his "hardcore" self-image. She also thinks the engineer identity helps him justify his lack of empathy. In her view he engineers the reality around him to match what he's selling.

## "Big lies" and reward hacking (37:20–40:50)

Swisher and Gibney agree that Musk's greatest invention is himself. His cult of personality lets him defy economic fundamentals.

- Gibney says it started with fibs and grew into big lies. He drew on Ed Niedermeyer's book *Ludicrous* about the gap between what Musk said Tesla was and what it actually was.
- He credits Musk with a real talent for risk and for crafting stories that unlock equity markets.
- **Reward hacking:** Gibney's main example is Tesla's battery-swap demonstration. Musk claimed a car's huge battery could be swapped in minutes, which Gibney calls ludicrous. Doing it once qualified Tesla for large California tax incentives.
- Tesla employees laughed when Gibney asked whether Musk was a great engineer, but they wouldn't say so on camera.
- When the fundamentals don't justify the stock, the "Elon as genius" story does.

## "Elon time" (40:50–43:20)

Swisher asks why real achievements like electric cars and rockets weren't enough, and why everything had to escalate to living on Mars.

Justine describes Musk as impatient, with a different sense of time. She mentions his old joke "safety third" and worries about that attitude going into the AI era. Other people's pain is treated as collateral damage and dismissed as "just feelings." He avoids areas where his strengths aren't on display.

## Errol Musk (43:19–48:37)

Swisher notes that Musk has called his father a terrible human being. She also cites a *New York Times* report that Errol was accused of sexually abusing five of his children.

**Justine's account:**

- There's a lot of silence about Errol in the family. At first she assumed Elon's mother, Maye, was exaggerating the stories about him.
- When Errol visited them in Palo Alto, he was charming at first. Elon said Errol could behave for about three days before turning mean.
- What struck her was that when Errol got insulting, he used the same language Elon used to insult her.
- Relatives used to try to rein Elon in by telling him he was acting like Errol, which was the thing he least wanted to be.

**Gibney's account:**

- He included Errol because "the apple doesn't fall far from the tree." Errol insists he and Elon are alike.
- He acknowledges Elon suffered psychological abuse from his father, which Kimbal also describes.
- The film includes audio of Kimbal saying you can't believe anything their father says. Gibney notes the irony that Elon became a gaslighter himself.
- He describes an uncomfortable interview in which Errol recounts killing intruders and dwells on the colors of the blood, as if still relishing the violence.

## Jenna and Musk's transphobia (48:37–55:50)

- Justine saw early on that Elon and Jenna were "on a collision course." She sent him long texts urging him to accept Jenna. He dismissed them as "maternal spam."
- She wanted the children to have the best possible relationship with their father.
- Musk later said in a Jordan Peterson interview that he had been "tricked" into signing Jenna's consent form. Justine felt it was aimed at her, possibly as bait. She says his original decision to sign had actually made her respect him more.
- She was confused by his claims to be socially progressive, since he often didn't seem to care.
- Swisher recalls Musk calling her late at night years earlier, promising to fight a Trump executive order targeting LGBTQ people. She asks whether any of that was true.
- Justine guesses the "woke mind virus" story is a way for him to resolve his own cognitive dissonance. She calls it an "origin story" for his rise as a Republican, and it was a "WTF moment" for her.
- In her view, Jenna rejected her father because he first rejected who she was. The price of being around him was too high. Justine says she and others leave time with him feeling terrible about themselves without knowing why.

Gibney says a child breaking free of a parent's control is normal, but Musk couldn't accept it. Because nothing can be merely personal for him, he turned it into a larger story and used Jenna as a foil to explain his political shift.

## Why Musk went MAGA (55:50–1:06:45)

Gibney and Swisher argue the real reasons were transactional:

- the threat a Kamala Harris win posed to him
- the many civil investigations into his companies, some of which have since been dropped

Swisher adds other factors:

- his anger at Biden over being snubbed at an EV event
- becoming "extremely online" after buying Twitter in 2022 and falling into conspiracy theories
- reported drug use

**On DOGE:**

- Musk spent nearly $300 million helping elect Trump. DOGE was the reward. It cost more than it saved, and Musk later called the government "basically unfixable."
- Gibney argues DOGE was never about waste. It was about weakening government so there would be fewer limits on people like Musk.
- He says the DOGE staff didn't understand the agencies they entered but were sophisticated about one thing: getting "super admin" access to agency data, which is deliberately kept separate to protect the public.
- He says he can't prove the data went to SpaceX, but moving it would be trivial. Citing Geoffrey Hinton and *Wired*'s Katie Drummond, he raises the possibility of AI being trained on that data and the risk to the midterms.
- He says there's always a "bank shot" with Musk. Twitter was bought half on a dare and then became a personal propaganda machine. Swisher adds that Mark Cuban told her at the time that the purchase was about influence.

**Justine's view:** Musk saw Trump as someone he could work with. She mentions Jeffrey Epstein as a "ghost in the background." She says Epstein's views on women overlap with Errol's, and that extended outward they form a worldview that seems like the only sensible way to live. In that view, only these men can save us from an apocalyptic future. Swisher adds that they treat ordinary people as "NPCs," a favorite term of Musk's, and casts them as messiahs.

**What it all adds up to:**

- Swisher notes Musk was one of the earliest tech figures to warn about AI, yet he is building his own through xAI and Grok.
- Gibney's film links Musk's businesses: AI, SpaceX, Starlink, Tesla's cameras and microphones, and government data.
- Gibney's biggest fear is that Musk seems psychologically unhinged and has enormous power he doesn't know what to do with. In the film, someone asks Musk why he needs to bring all this power together into one global business, and he can't answer. Gibney compares it to Ted Kennedy famously failing to explain why he was running for president.
- Gibney says the film tries to convey both how dangerous Musk's power is and how fundamentally unserious he is.
- Asked whether Musk is a calculating genius or a chaos agent, Justine says he can jump off a cliff and invent the parachute on the way down. She ties his constant life-or-death urgency to what she has read about PTSD. He brings chaos with him and pulls others in. She assumes there's method in it, with plenty of sleight of hand.
- She also suggests that once you've engineered rockets, the next step is engineering beliefs and human minds, which she calls a classic science-fiction idea.

## Legacy (1:06:45–end)

*New York Times* reporter Kirsten Grind asks whether Musk's conduct now outweighs his contributions.

**Gibney** says yes, the damage outweighs the good, especially DOGE.

- He credits Musk with giving Tesla and SpaceX a sense of mission that attracted talented engineers and executives. Those people did much of the real work.
- He rejects the "take the bad with the good" view he attributes to Walter Isaacson's biography. Musk's cruelty, he says, has done "cataclysmic" damage to the country.

**Justine** says Musk is often guilty of what he accuses others of. For example, when he announced he was a Republican, he called Democrats the party of division and hate. She wonders if he is playing to his strengths as a "war god," who needs a war to be needed.

**Do we know him?**

- Gibney says we now understand the levers Musk pulls to create his Wizard-of-Oz image, but not what he really believes, or whether he can be honest with himself.
- Justine says she used to think she knew him well. Looking back, you can see early signs of where he ended up, and she wonders about the moments when he could have gone a different way. Swisher agrees; she thinks about that often.
- Justine says Musk can't stand boredom and needs giant problems. As tech and space lift him further from ordinary life, people start to look like toys in his toy chest. She thinks "trickster" fits him.

**How does it end?**

- Gibney doesn't know. A lot depends on the coming midterms and what happens when Trump leaves power. He echoes Katie Drummond's concern that Musk may have gathered too much power for the pendulum to swing back.
- Justine fears a new kind of *1984*, with eyes everywhere. She cites Einstein's line that imagination matters more than knowledge. The challenge to men like Musk, she says, is to imagine a future different from the past, not another fallen empire. Swisher adds "Ozymandias" and "Babylon was."

The show notes that Musk did not respond to a request for comment.
