1. Watch Isaac Asimov talks about superstition, religion and why he teaches rationality, 1988, 24m43s.

Or you can read a cleaned up transcript of the video.

  • Asimov was a great American science fiction writer (among the many other things he wrote about). He was born in Russia in 1919 or 1920. This connects him to the Russian lineage that runs from Notes from Underground to the science-fiction dystopian novel We, written in Russia in about 1920–1921, which Orwell read before writing 1984. Virtually all science fiction writers after him, such as those for The Matrix and The Perfect Match, have been affected by his work.

  • In Class 4, we briefly discussed Asimov’s famous Three Laws of Robotics, which date to 1942 or so.
    By assuming the robots in his stories were bound by these rules, he was able to craft stories that draw people in by means other than the primary emotion of fear. The three laws are:

    1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
    2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
    3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

  • The interviewer of Asimov in the video is Richard Douglas Heffner, the creator and host of The Open Mind, a public affairs television show first broadcast in 1956.

  • Asimov responds to Heffner’s mention of Brave New World and 1984.

    • He says that the fact that these stories are dystopian “makes for better drama.”

  • Asimov brings up the equation “2+2=4”.

    • He says (obviously a bit metaphorically) that most people would prefer to be told with certainty that 2+2=5 than to hear that 2+2=4 with equivocation and uncertainty.

  • In the video, he presents a case for science as a unifying force.

  • Optional: Watch The Atheist Who Wrote 1,300 Pages Explaining The Bible | Isaac Asimov, YouTube, 16:44.

    • The video presents an interesting example of two great public intellectuals, in full agreement on content, taking different strategic approaches to communication. Asimov once signed a collective letter bluntly stating that astrology is bogus, whereas Carl Sagan thought that such a letter was the wrong instrument for making progress. Better, Sagan thought, was to publish rebuttals of specific claims.

Questions on the Asimov interview

  • How essential is rationality for a modern, powerful, wealthy country?

  • “Alignment” is a fashionable tech term. An AI is in alignment when its operational goals agree with those of its creators. This brings up many questions:

    • Can we trust the goals of the creators?
    • Will it be possible to constrain AI to follow its creators’ explicit instructions and implicit desires?
    • Is some sort of overarching moral constraint on AI possible, something like Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics?
