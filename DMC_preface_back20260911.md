## 1. Genesis of the Course


I do not think I am going out on a limb by thinking that everyone taking this course has been observing the American and world political scene for decades.
A line that comes to mind is, “When I think of the road we’re traveling on, I wonder what’s gone wrong. I can’t help it. I wonder what’s gone wrong.”[^americantune]


[^americantune]: Quoted lyric from "[[American Tune]{.underline}](https://genius.com/Paul-simon-american-tune-lyrics)" by Paul Simon.

Granted, not all of you will be obsessed with the dark side. Some know that focusing on the positive is a better way of making a hopeful prophecy self-fulfilling. But for my purposes in trying to control you to be receptive to what I have to say, let us assume that all of you have, at least inchoately, been looking for some time at politics through the lens of mind control.

All of you may have some contradictory senses of what that means, but you might be hoping that I will offer at least a provisional definition of what I mean by it. First, let me distinguish between constructive and destructive mind control, between individual and systemic mind control, and between threatening and enticing mind control.

### Constructive vs. Destructive Mind Control

Constructive mind control offers options to improve how we think and feel. Think of an inspiring teacher whose goal is not to bedazzle students, but to get them to think for themselves.

Destructive mind control, on the other hand, manipulates people into thinking within a rigid framework that harms its recipients in the long run. 
Think about cults[^hassan], brainwashing prisoners of war[^lemov], or dictators who rule by fear or spin[^spin].
Or think about people who crowd out all reason by mindlessly repeating clichés and slogans, filling our minds and media with bullshit[^stupid].
Speaking of media, the proliferation of media today (social media, blogs, ..., and now AI) exerts tremendous control on the kind of messages we communicate[^mcluhan].


[^hassan]: See [[Steven Hassan’s books on cults]{.underline}](https://stevenhassan.freedomofmind.com/?utm_campaign=profile_chips).
[^lemov]: See [[*The Instability of Truth*]{.underline}](https://wwnorton.com/books/9781324075264)  by Rebecca Lemov. 
[^spin]:    See [[*Spin Dictators*]{.underline}](https://spindictators.com)  by Sergei Guriev & Daniel Treisman.
[^stupid]:  See [[*Stupid Idiots*]{.underline}](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/S/bo266710060.html) by Lars Svendsen
             and [[*On Bullshit*]{.underline}](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691276786/on-bullshit) by Harry G. Frankfurt.
[^mcluhan]: See [[*The Medium is the Message*]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_medium_is_the_message)  by Marshall McLuhan.

### Individual vs.  Systemic Mind Control

Systemic, or system-wide, mind control is what is imposed upon us—either for good or bad—by society. It is structural. It is the water we swim in that we cannot always even see. Think, for example, of [[the phrase “under God” being inserted into the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954]{.underline}]( https://www.history.com/articles/pledge-allegiance-under-god-schools) as part of the Cold War against atheist communists.

Individual mind control is what friends, family, acquaintances, our boss, our pastor, our therapist, or our teachers do to try to influence us. At the most personal level, it is what we ourselves do to try to control our own minds. This ranges from impulsively eating chocolate to perk ourselves up, to hours of silent meditation to calm the demons within. In modern parlance, it is the “executive function” decision not to be overwhelmed by anxiety caused by that bad math teacher[^studentapology] and instead to spend a fixed amount of time each day doing our best to learn before taking a break for a healthy diversion[^selfapology].


[^studentapology]:  Apologies to my former students if I have afflicted you this way.
[^selfapology]: Apologies to myself for the times I have obsessively not followed this strategy.

Since I am all too often a glass-half-empty kind of guy, I have a mathematician’s obsession with looking for patterns, 
and I have a penchant for theories of everything;
the kind of mind control I have worried about for a long time is destructive and systemic. For purposes of our course, it is the default version of mind control that we will mostly focus on here.

In real life, the dichotomies between destructive and constructive, and between individual and systemic, may be useful but are actually quite fuzzy. I hope you will help me break out of my own controlled brain and explore these distinctions rather than use them rigidly.


### Threatening vs.  Enticing Mind Control

Mind control based on threats, or implicit threats, manipulates people based on when negative emotions like fear and hatred.  You could call this mind control of a stick. The systemic and usually destructive version of this often takes the form of a “fear dictator” who rules by threats and punishment.  He can also take the form of a more modern “spin dictator”, rules by controlling information flow.

On the other hand, enticing, mind, control  controls people, the allure of comfort or riches.  The systemic and destructive version of this mind control with a carrot, can take the form of mowing a population into complacency by not only providing for their needs, but by creating a dependency on “false needs”.


### More Features of Mind Control Instances

Any particular example of  mind control will have one of the possible values of each of the above feature, or perhaps be a weighted combination
of the different possibilities.

There are many other features that can be used to characterize the nature of instances of mind control.
The features are correlated, i.e. having one value of a particular feature says something about the likelihood for the value of another feature.

For example, if a particular case of mind control works by threat, it is more likely to be destructive as well.

There are many other  features of the examples of mind control that we consider.

For example, the **extremist vs balanced** feature gauges whether the controller is always pushing a swinging pendulum 
towards the extreme  in one direction vs. nudging towards a balance equilibrium.
With this imagery in mind, perhaps you fear that swinging pendulum of our politics is being pushed to extremes to hard that it may just spin over the top,
with unpredictable consequences.


Another feature  measure wheter a mind control instance is intended to make people **stop thought** or if it is meant to  **enourage thought**.
Generally, we may think that stopping thought is likely to be destructive, although it can certainly be the case that encourage bad thought, or
encouraging people to stew in their own mind is destructive as well.

The *simplicity vs complexity* feature gaugues whether a mind control instance pushes toward black-and-white simplicity or whether 
it encourage people to engage with complexity.   I think that different individual are more prone to being influenced to think one way or another.
Sometimes simplicity is useful to cut through noise, whereas oversimplification can encourage jumping to the wrong conclusion to soon[^holmessimpicity].
The tendency to stop thought is probably highly correlated with the tendency to oversimplify.

[^holmessimpicity]: One of my favorite quote is by Oliver Wendell Holmes, Senior, the father of the supreme court justice: “For the simplicity on this side of complexity, I wouldn't give you a fig. But for the simplicity on the other side of complexity, for that I would give you anything I have.”

As you can see from the kind of data analysis approach of the prevous section, I am backing down from trying to find solutions to simply trying

I must admit that my thinking about mind control has been biased by intense interactions I have had with individuals,
and by the particular slice of media I consume. So there are particular notions about mind control that I tend to focus on, 
as well as myths I believe others are overattached to.

A course—not this course—that I have fantasized about putting together would be a kind of philosophical list of do's and dont’s for a mature thinker thinking about politics, religion, science, and life, with examples chosen and motivated by my personal experience.

But when I started to think of putting together a real course—this course for BOLLI—I realized I needed to get out of my own personal information bubble in order to gain a broader perspective and avoid having the course degenerate into narrow commentary on current politics and personal grievances.

I think abstract themes are only useful if tested against evidence. Unfortunately, a consequence of the polarization our society is 
currently experiencing is that we cannot even agree on what facts are important, or even what facts are true.
So, I decided to step back—or you could call it a retreat—to the world of fiction, where at least we all have equal access to the core facts: those presented in the story being focused on. The heart of this course will be the sequence of stories we will all be reading or watching, discussion questions to get us focused on the same thing, and short quotes from the stories so that we are starting from the same place.

I will suggest that the stories fit into an interpretive arc and suggest some tools to help make sense of that interpretation. The interpretations and tools I suggest are just that: suggestions. 

All of these  stories and tools have been the focus of a full course, or more.
To make the course more accessible, as a sort of introductory sampler, we will focus on three films and a short story.
The primary goal will be to encounter the stories in their own terms.
The tools I introduce are meant to foster thinking and discussion, not present a barrier of abstractions.
They are my suggestions of some types of sand castles we might work on together in our classroom sandbox.

I look forward to hearing your arguments, alternatives, and even complaints.

## 3. What We Will Be Exploring

I call the four stories we will consider, taken together,  *an* arc of dystopian stories. Each story represents a kind of dystopia in which systemic forces are used to destructively control the minds of people in society. The protagonists in each story represent hero figures struggling to see the light. The stories tell tales of attempted defiance.

<!-- I will try not to give away the endings in this preface, but be prepared: -->

The stories are dystopian in that each involve a kind of destructive mind control pervasively imposed by the world in which the protaganist lives.
I will sometimes refer to the protaganists as "heroes", in the sense that I am rooting for them in their attempts to resist mind control, 
not in the sense of foreshadowing that they will succeed.

The kind of mind control the hero is struggling against progresses from story to story, with an evolving, increasingly sophisticated,  mix of fear and enticement techniques, which enabled by  increasingly powerful, soft and hard techniques.
The techniques of control are cumulative. 
Just because a new technique evolves, doesn't mean the old ones disappear.
In fact, we are struggling today with the techniques in all of the stories in the arc we will consider.

I italicized the first word of "*an* arc of dystopian stories" to make clear that these stories are simply the ones I have selected to
demonstrate how the mind control techniques evolve.
Sadly, there are many other prophetic choices we could pick.
My hope is not that you will think of this arc as some sort of canon, but as an example of how you might get 
perspective by putting together an arc of stories that speak to you from literature, or your personal life.

As the techniques of dystopian mind control evolve, the tools we use to look at them co-evolve.
You could think of them  "*an* arc of lenses", giving a sample of evolving ways to look at stories.

### Tale 1: *Notes from Underground*

Our first story, *Notes from Underground* by Fyodor Dostoevsky, takes place at a time I am pegging as the start of the “modern age.” The story was first published in Russian in 1864 and takes place in Petersburg around 1862–1864, as the American Civil War is raging and as people in Russia are struggling with whether and how to absorb the lessons of the Western Enlightenment.

The unnamed protagonist of the story, often referred to as Dostoevsky’s Underground Man, is in many ways an antihero. What he is defying seems to be the Enlightenment itself and sane society. If we try to understand the Underground Man, perhaps we can better understand our fellow citizens today who seem to want to roll back to a time before Enlightenment values. Perhaps we can learn to accept that they are, in many ways, crazy but also have an important insights.

One tool we will introduce could be called human outlier analysis[^madness_and_civilization].

[^madness_and_civilization]: See *[Madness and Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madness_and_Civilization)* by Michel Foucault.
This 1961 book posits that a phenomenon of the Age of Reason
is the attempt to eliminate the irrational from philosophical discourse.
Institutions (such as hospitals and prisons) were developed to outcast radical thinkers as mad or criminal.
Many of us today might see the Underground Man as crazy in his verbose rambling and perverse actions.
He may seems preposterous and confused, but do we lose something important if he is ostracized as a mental defective?

We will ask questions about the Underground Man that could well be asked of ourselves and those around us:

- *How is the protaganist unusual in the society they live, and how are they trying to influence society? *  
- *Do you sympathize with the character and his plight?*  
- *Can we useful separate out the mental illness from the creative insight?*  
- *What does he see about society that those around him do not see?*



### Tale 2: *1984*

Our next story is *1984* by George Orwell, published in 1949, after World War II was over, just as society was struggling with how to 
face weapons of mass destruction: physical nuclear weapons, global technological warfare, and mental totalitarian control of communications.

We are all familiar with many references to how aspects of our current society are “Orwellian,” with its police state[^foucault_panopticon],
which is a form of controlling people who are outliers.

[^foucault_panopticon]: See
[Discipline & Punish (1975), Panopticism](https://files.libcom.org/files/michel-foucault-panopticism.pdf) by Michel Foucault:   
-- "The Panopticon is a marvellous machine which, whatever use one may wish to put it to, produces homogeneous effects of power."  
-- "[T]he Panopticon was also a laboratory; it could be used as a machine to carry out experiments, to alter behaviour, to train or correct individuals"


Many of us feel the constant stress of narratives being imposed upon us, often without our conscious knowledge.
Like the fish who does not have a concept for water, we may be unaware of how our thoughts are constrained by the hiding of evidence -- see Orwell’s memory hole in *1984* -- and by the limitations placed on our very ability to speak -- see Orwell’s language of Newspeak, referred to in the movie and also explained in detail in the appendix to the book.

As a class, we also must be aware that Orwell’s tale is so powerful because he has constructed a narrative of his own creation, brilliantly written to focus on possibilities he sees with prophetic vision. We will try to engage in a kind of narrative analysis[^bruner_narrative_reconstruction].

[^bruner_narrative_reconstruction]: See [“The Narrative Construction of Reality” (Critical Inquiry, Vol. 18, No. 1, Autumn 1991)](https://www.academia.edu/9503047/The_Narrative_Construction_of_Reality) and ["Bruner's Narrative Theory for Defiance Course"](../references/DMC_bruner_narrative_theory.pdf).


We will ask questions such as:

-  *Mental control often attempts to flatten a person’s inner life. How does the narrative use the "intentional states" (private thoughts) of the characters to defy the external world?*
- *In a "mentally controlled world," the system provides the "canonical script" (how one should think and act). How does the protagonist of our story create a "breach" in that script?*
- *In propaganda-heavy worlds, "truth" is whatever the state says. How does a "tale of defiance" use "narrative truth"to expose the lies of "systemic truth"?*
- *Why do we often use the "Dystopian" genre to talk about mental control? How does the genre itself help us understand what "defiance" looks like?*
- *How do individual acts of defiance "accrue" into a movement?*


### Tale 3: *The Matrix*

Next, in class three, we will focus on the 1999 movie *The Matrix*. The mind control here is total control of reality itself. Our hero, Neo, chooses to swallow the “red pill,” which lets him see that the entire world he has been living in is an elaborate computer simulation.

This movie is prophetic of the encroachment of virtual reality on our present-day world. 

A tool here is to examine how society is being controlled[^marcuse].

[^marcuse]: 
See [Herbert Marcuse’s notion of societies of control]().
Also see [From 1984 to One-Dimensional Man: Critical Reflections on Orwell and Marcuse](../references/kellner_from1984_to_marcuse_onedimensional.pdf)

We will ask questions such as:

- *Who or what is controlling society?*
- *How is it being controlled?*
