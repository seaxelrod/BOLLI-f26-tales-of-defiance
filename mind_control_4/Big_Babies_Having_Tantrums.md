## Preface: From Obsession with Cults to Constructive Thinking

It is easy for me to obsess about the systemic, destructive use of mind control in our public discourse. I am all too often a glass-half-empty kind of guy, and I have a mathematician's habit of looking for patterns everywhere. Put those together and I can find cult-like, even brainwashing, influence almost anywhere I look, including in mainstream media, religion, and science.

Some of what I see is real. Populists, like the spin dictators many of them admire, use partial truths to encourage limited, ideological thinking. That kind of thinking is more likely to reinforce belief in myths, old and newly invented, than to encourage real critical thinking. This is not to say that emotion should be ruled out. Emotions are often the driver. But the power to drive people with emotion is a dangerous one, and it must be handled with care.

Seeing the pattern everywhere is also a trap. When I started putting together a course, I realized I needed to get out of my own information bubble, or the course would degenerate into narrow commentary on current politics and personal grievances. Our polarized society cannot even agree on which facts matter, or which facts are true. So I stepped back from my particular cultural here and now (you could call it a retreat) to the world of fiction, where at least we all have equal access to the facts of the story. The result is a little course in my senior community, at BOLLI, called "Tales of Defiance to Mind Control in the Modern Age." We follow an arc of dystopian stories, from Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground* through Orwell's *1984* and *The Matrix* to Ken Liu's "The Perfect Match," each about a protagonist trying to resist the mind control imposed by the world around them.

In that course I distinguish constructive from destructive mind control. Constructive mind control offers options for improving how we think and feel. Think of an inspiring teacher whose goal is not to dazzle students but to get them to think for themselves. Destructive mind control manipulates people into thinking within a rigid framework that harms them in the long run. One telltale sign of the destructive kind is that it pushes people toward black-and-white simplicity and away from complexity. A favorite saying of mine, attributed to Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr., is that the simplicity on this side of complexity isn't worth a fig, while the simplicity on the other side of complexity is worth anything.

An essential question for the final week of the course is "What have we learned, and where do we go from here?" This essay is a partial answer, for myself, about where I might want to go. For a long time I hoped to find a magic formula for overcoming systemic destructive mind control. I have backed down from that to looking for the right questions, and for good company in asking them.

I acknowledge that I am driven both by things I see as problems and by my need to feel good about helping to provide solutions. Part III says more about that. But here is why I think the drive is worth acting on rather than suppressing. Autocrats want to isolate us and make us feel powerless in that isolation, unable to join together into a movement. One of the questions we ask about *1984* is what helps or hurts the ability of individual acts of defiance to accrue into a movement. There is a danger when we leave thought leadership to those whose main goal is to accumulate power over people's minds. I hope to contribute to a culture where our still, small voices can collaborate for the common good on a small stage, even as the role models who deserve it do so on a large stage.

Not all of us have a secret desire to be a thought leader. But I hope we can restore a democratic system that supports a thoughtful electorate, one that makes wise decisions collectively.

As an aside, it has been easy for me to get carried away with the metaphysical problem of understanding the "underlying" nature of reality. *The Matrix*, and the simulation theories it helped popularize, can pull me right in. But that pursuit should be constrained by a practical question: "How is this productive?" The essay that follows is my attempt to stay on the productive side of that question.

## Part I: Handling Big Babies Having Tantrums

Heather Cox Richardson's [November 17, 2026 *Letters from an American*](https://heathercoxrichardson.substack.com/p/november-17-2026) describes IT security teams scrambling to fix software vulnerabilities revealed by AI, and the widespread tech failures that are following. She also describes how the Trump administration, and DOGE in particular, has systematically fired the government employees whose job it is to help prevent and fix exactly these kinds of systemic problems.

This has got me thinking about how to frame the problem in a simple way, one that might help nudge people away from bad ideas that have been accumulating power for many, many years.

It's hard, complex work to run the most advanced country in the world. Bogus populist ideologies that lead to dismantling what is good and pitting people against each other are tearing us apart. The US is fond of the metaphor of "war," as in "the war on terror." But a war on an ideology is quite different from a war on a group of people. And war is the opposite of a collective search for useful ways of comprehending the world, which is our best proxy for truth.

We have to get over the reflex to jump to simple ideas that promise short-term solutions to complex problems, without considering their obvious flaws. And we need to be able to hold people who do bad things accountable, while at the same time confronting the ideology that enabled them.

America has often flirted with running itself on the cowboy myth: the rugged individual who solves social problems with daring and guns, in a world where anyone who hampers the cowboy is an enemy, and no problem is more complex than taking down the enemy. Even more of the country is lured in by parallels to the lone cowboy: the lone priest, the lone CEO, the lone podcaster. In general, we are attracted to figures who comfort us with the thought that the world is not too complicated.

The cowboy ethos can at times solve local problems. But it has proven a disaster now that a coalition of forces has allied under a populist banner. That banner works against our constitutional principle, which places more faith in a government of elected, legally accountable people, whose power is checked and who work together for the common good, than in a country of the rich, by the rich, and for the rich.

Many a con man has tricked people into trusting him by putting on a false persona. Populists elevate the con man's tactics to win the trust of the masses. Our democracy depends on voters collectively being able to weed these people out. To do that, it depends on a trusted, well-functioning knowledge-processing ecosystem. It requires media, institutions of learning, judicial processes, and industry leaders who work for the broad-based common good, and who are driven more by genuine morality and a search for the best available truth than by a narrow, self-serving ideology.

We have much work to do. Let us not reward those who exploit animosity to gain power. We can't let them convince us to blame the people who are honestly trying to solve the hard problems the exploiters created.

In short, let us not be governed by big babies having tantrums.

## Part II: Standing on the Shoulders of Adult Giants

Isaac Newton wrote to Robert Hooke in 1675 that if he had seen further, it was by standing on the shoulders of giants. He was borrowing an image that goes back at least to Bernard of Chartres in the twelfth century, which is itself an example of the point: even the line about building on others was built on others.

The giants I have in mind are adults in a specific sense. They did the patient, unglamorous work of figuring out how people can solve hard problems *together*, without waiting for a hero to ride into town. Here are some of the shoulders I am trying to stand on.

**Designing government for imperfect people.** James Madison wrote in *Federalist* No. 51 that "if men were angels, no government would be necessary," and that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." The constitutional principle I appeal to in Part I is his: don't count on good rulers; build a system of checked, accountable power that works even when some of the people in it are not good. In *Federalist* No. 10 he worried about factions, groups united by a passion or interest opposed to the common good, which is close to what I mean by a populist coalition. My phrase "of the rich, by the rich, and for the rich" is, of course, Lincoln's Gettysburg Address turned inside out.

**Democracy as collective inquiry.** John Dewey, in *The Public and Its Problems* (1927), treated democracy as the way a public discovers its shared problems and works them out through communication and inquiry, rather than as a contest to be won. Karl Popper, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), argued that since none of us has the final truth, the best societies are the ones that let us find and correct our mistakes, including getting rid of bad leaders without bloodshed. He preferred piecemeal fixes that can be tested and revised over grand plans that can't. Jonathan Rauch, in *The Constitution of Knowledge* (2021), describes the network of journalists, scientists, courts, and scholars whose shared rules for checking claims let a society turn disagreement into knowledge. His picture is the fuller version of what I call a knowledge-processing ecosystem.

**Solving problems without a lone hero.** Elinor Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel Prize in economics for showing, in *Governing the Commons* (1990) and later work, that ordinary communities can manage shared resources (fisheries, forests, irrigation systems) by building and enforcing their own rules. She showed that the choice is not only between a strongman and a free-for-all. People who keep talking to each other can govern themselves.

**Seeing the simple-answer trap.** H. L. Mencken warned in 1917 that there is always a well-known solution to every human problem: "neat, plausible, and wrong." Richard Hofstadter's *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life* (1963) traces how distrust of expertise has run through American politics and religion from the start, so today's version is not new. George Lakoff's essay "Metaphor and War" (1991) shows how a metaphor like war quietly decides which solutions look reasonable before any argument is made.

**Witnesses to the present.** Michael Lewis's *The Fifth Risk* (2018) profiles the experts who keep the federal government running. The "fifth risk" of his title is the risk a society runs when it falls into the habit of responding to long-term risks with short-term solutions. Heather Cox Richardson, besides the letter that started me off, has [traced how the myth of "cowboy individualism"](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/ideas/heather-cox-richardson-phil-lend-lecture-1.7522817) became a tool for persuading voters to dismantle government programs that benefited them. Daniel Drezner's *The Toddler in Chief* (2020) is the scholarly cousin of my title. His key point is that a leader's childish behavior becomes dangerous mainly when the checks on that leader have eroded, which brings us back to Madison.

None of these people is a lone cowboy. Each depended on and argued with others, and each left something that others could build on. That is what I mean by an adult giant.

## Part III: Some Introspection about Figuring It Out for Ourselves

What I have written above is a personal snapshot. It is my attempt to pull together things that are in the air right now, along with my own small efforts to make progress in that direction. Another person, or I myself a year from now, might put the pieces together differently.

I should admit what I want. I want to be part of a movement that reaches many people, not just a few friends who already agree with me. That puts me close to something I criticized in Part I, because populists also want to move the masses. The difference I am trying to hold onto is in the means. I don't want to win people over by flattering their grievances, offering them an enemy, or promising that the world is simpler than it is. I want to invite people into the slow, shared work of figuring things out, even when that is less exciting.

I should also admit that my ego wants something here. I want to feel that I am making a contribution. That is not a bad motive, but it can bias me. It can tempt me to promote my own pet ideas and framings, like "big babies" and "adult giants," over other people's ideas that are better. A movement built around one person's favorite slogans starts to look like the lone-cowboy problem in a new hat.

The image of standing on the shoulders of giants cuts both ways. From up there you can peek over the giant's head and see a little further than the giant could. But you only got there because the giant carried you. The view is a gift, not an achievement, and what you report back should be offered with humility, ready to be corrected by the next person who climbs up.

So I offer this essay in that spirit: as one person's attempt to see a bit further, using borrowed height, and open to being shown where I got it wrong.

## Appendix: Conceptual Template of This Essay

The essay follows a simple five-step template that could be reused for other topics:

* **Assess concerns.** Here: trying to figure out how to be a small-pond thought leader, and looking for a direction to go after teaching a mini-course on "Tales of Defiance to Mind Control in the Modern Age."
* **Point to a problem.** Here: being governed by big babies having tantrums, meaning leaders and movements that offer simple, angry answers to complex problems and tear down the institutions that solve them.
* **Point to a suggestion.** Here: stand on the shoulders of adult giants. Draw on people who have already done serious work on how to solve problems collectively.
* **Reflect on the process.** Here: introspect about how we figure things out for ourselves, including our own motives and biases, so that the suggestion doesn't turn into another version of the problem.
* **Assess patterns.** Here: this appendix, which steps back to name the shape of the essay so it can be reused, questioned, or improved.
